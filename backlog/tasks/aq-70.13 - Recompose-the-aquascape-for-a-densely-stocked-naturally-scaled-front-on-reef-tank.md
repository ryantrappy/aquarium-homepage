## Professional aquascaping best practices (research 2026-11-09)

Sources: Reef Central, "The Fundamentals of Aquascaping" (Takashi Amano's golden section); aquairi.app, "Aquascaping Composition: Rule of Thirds, Golden Ratio and Focal Points"; Trends Magazine, "How to Design a Visually Balanced and Functional Reef Aquascape"; Reef Co Aquariums, "2026 Aquascaping Trends: Biotope, Naturalistic, and Minimalist Designs"; AlgaeBarn, "Designing Saltwater Tanks for Behavior Harmony"; My First Fish Tank, "Reef Aquascaping Guide: Build a Stable Saltwater Rockscape"; Charterhouse Aquatics, "Coral Zoning & Aquascape Layout in UNS Reef Systems"; Reef Builders, "How to Properly Place Corals in a New Reef Tank"; Pacific East Aquaculture, lighting/placement guides; Reef2Reef and Bay Area Reefers community build and feedback threads.

### Composition
- Rule of thirds / golden section: the dominant element sits on a thirds intersection or about 3/5 across the tank, never dead centre. Choose a single focal point; multiple competing focal points cancel each other out.
- Asymmetric islands, not a wall: two or three separate rock islands/bommies with a valley or swim lane between them. A continuous rock wall reads as a pile, blocks flow, and traps detritus.
- Negative space is a feature, not leftover: keep at least ~20% of the volume as open swimming water, and keep the foreground sand bed open. "Less rock, more impact" — open sand and open water around the rockwork create the contrast that makes the reef read large.

### Structure and height
- Height variation and terracing: slope low at the front and higher toward the back; terraced ledges stepping down toward the sand. Leave vertical headroom above the rock so coral colonies have room to grow into it.
- Arches, caves, swim-throughs and overhangs: at least one arch or swim-through, caves at sand level, overhangs and shelves for future coral placement. They give fish pathways and territories, break lines of sight between territorial fish, and read as depth from the front.
- Flow channels: no solid walls. Leave channels for water to move through and around the rock so every zone gets flow and detritus does not collect.

### Layering and coral zoning (light and flow)
- Three zones. Upper reef (high light, high flow): SPS such as Acropora, Montipora digitata, Stylophora, Pocillopora. Mid reef (moderate light/flow): LPS such as Euphyllia torch/hammer, Blastomussa, Duncan. Lower reef (reduced light, gentle flow): soft corals, zoanthids, mushrooms, chalices; fleshy LPS on the sandbed or low protected ledges.
- Spacing: at least 2–3 inches between species to prevent aggression and sweeper-tentacle damage; fast-growing corals go low so they do not overshadow slow growers above them.
- Foreground / midground / background: low encrusting and sandbed corals in front, branching and LPS mass in the middle, taller SPS and a back-rock layer behind for depth.

### Color and texture
- Complementary contrast, not clustering: place green against purple, orange against blue, red against green. Do not cluster all of one color together — individual colonies lose their identity.
- Mix textures: smooth leather/encrusting forms next to fine branching staghorn and fleshy LPS; varied textures create depth perception and invite the eye to explore.
- One dominant color-pop colony per island (for example a saturated red gorgonian or neon-green staghorn) against darker rock, with actinic fluorescence as an accent, not a blanket wash.

### Growth, maintenance and fish
- Leave growth room around every colony and clearance from the glass for cleaning; base rock sits on the tank bottom before sand so burrows cannot undermine the structure.
- Frame the fish: long open swimming lanes in front of and above the reef for schooling and open-water fish, plus caves, crevices and shaded areas for shy, territorial species. The scape should support natural behavior, not just look good.
